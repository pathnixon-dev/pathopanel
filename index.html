PathoPanel
Morphology first. Then the stain that answers the question.

Choose the stain that separates your differential, not the stain that names your hunch.

PathoPanel asks about morphology one feature at a time, re-ranks a differential after every answer, and works out which antibody would do most to separate what is left. You enter the stains you actually ran, and it moves again.

Morphology → differential → the most informative stain → interpretation → reflex testing only when a question remains.

A teaching tool for pathology training. It does not make diagnoses, and its percentages are relative to its own library rather than to any patient.

About this tool

What it does

PathoPanel takes a morphological description one feature at a time and maintains a ranked differential over a library of tumour entities. After each answer it calculates which remaining question, and then which antibody, would do most to separate the diagnoses still in play. Stain results are entered back in and the differential moves again.

The purpose is to teach the order of reasoning rather than to supply lists. The question a resident should be asking is which stain answers the question in front of them, not which stains have ever been reported in a tumour.

What it holds, and how to use it

The library is organised into organ modules. The site you give routes the case to the right one, and each module asks its own questions. Soft tissue and central nervous system cases are asked about architecture, stroma and vasculature. Bone is asked about the matrix the lesion produces and what the radiology showed. Breast turns on whether a myoepithelial layer is identifiable, and skin on whether a lesion is symmetrical and maturing. Head and neck asks by subsite, since a parotid differential and a sinonasal one share almost nothing. The gastrointestinal and skin modules cover inflammatory and infective patterns as well as tumours, because much of that work is non-neoplastic.

Thirteen modules, holding 394 entities and 150 antibodies: soft tissue, central nervous system, bone, gynaecological, breast, gastrointestinal, head and neck, skin, pulmonary and pleural, genitourinary, hepatobiliary and pancreatic, endocrine, and cardiovascular.

The cardiovascular module behaves differently from the rest and should. Most of that work is decided on morphology and clinical correlation, so the tool will often reach a confident answer and then tell you that no remaining antibody would change it. That is the correct answer there, not a gap in the library.

You can step back and forward through a case at any point with the buttons or the arrow keys, and clicking any earlier answer in the case trail returns you to that point. Changing an answer discards what was ahead of it, because the next question is chosen from the differential and a different answer leads somewhere else.

How the numbers are produced

Every entity carries a distribution over the answers to each morphological question, and every antibody carries a probability of being positive in each entity. Answers and stain results update a prior over the library by likelihood. Questions and antibodies are ranked by expected reduction in entropy, which is why the tool sometimes recommends a stain that is negative in the leading diagnosis: a result that splits the field is more useful than a result that confirms what you already think.

What the percentages mean, and what they do not

They are relative to this library only. The figures describe how the tool weighs the entities it holds. They are not diagnostic probabilities, they are not calibrated against any case series, and they do not represent the likelihood that a patient has a disease.

A diagnosis outside the library cannot appear, but the tool will still look confident. This is the failure mode that matters. Given a case whose correct answer is not in the library, PathoPanel will still produce a ranked list with a leading diagnosis at seventy or eighty per cent. It has no way to say that the answer is elsewhere.

The weights are considered judgements, not published figures. Where a marker is given a value of 0.9 in an entity, that reflects the general strength of the association in standard diagnostic practice. It is not a sensitivity taken from a study, and it should not be quoted as one.

The library has not been verified against the current WHO classification. It was built from the three references below and cross-checked between them. Anyone using it to teach should confirm the entries against the relevant WHO volume first.

References

Entity selection, expected staining patterns and pitfalls were drawn from them and cross-checked against each other. All text in this tool was written fresh rather than reproduced, and none of their figures, tables or slide collections are included here. Where the sources disagreed, the more recent was followed, and the disagreements are recorded in the build notes.

  1. Rekhtman N, Bishop JA. Quick Reference Handbook for Surgical Pathologists. Springer, 2011. ISBN 978-3-642-20085-4. The current edition is Rekhtman N, Baine MK, Bishop JA, 2nd edition, Springer, 2019. Used for differential diagnosis tables, particularly the spindle cell, small round blue cell and central nervous system differentials.
  2. Schaberg KB. Immunohistochemistry. From "Kurt's Notes", teaching notes of the Department of Pathology, University of California, Davis. Revision of 29 January 2026. Freely available at schaberg.faculty.ucdavis.edu and kurtsnotes.net. Described in Schukow CP, Lee A, Cima L, Schaberg KB. "Kurt's Notes" as a free and high-yield worldwide surgical pathology e-learning resource. Int J Surg Pathol. 2025;33(2):426-429. PMID 38899890. Used for antibody localisation, current marker practice, and the principles that shaped how this tool recommends stains. The rule that a stain which will not change what you say is a stain not worth ordering is his, and it is the idea the whole ranking rests on.
  3. Gupta A. Ace the Boards: Surgical Pathology Reimagined. 2022. ISBN 979-8359829632. acemypath.com. Used for entity coverage and immunoprofiles in the bone, soft tissue and gynaecological modules.

Further reading

  • WHO Classification of Tumours Editorial Board. WHO Classification of Tumours, 5th edition. International Agency for Research on Cancer, 2019 onwards. The Blue Books are the gold standard for tumour diagnosis and set the international standards this tool should be checked against. The series is published volume by volume: digestive system and breast in 2019, thoracic in 2021, central nervous system and urinary and male genital in 2022, endocrine and neuroendocrine and skin in 2025, eye and orbit in 2026. tumourclassification.iarc.who.int
  • Goldblum JR, Lamps LW, McKenney JK, Myers JL. Rosai and Ackerman's Surgical Pathology. 11th edition, two volumes. Elsevier, 2018. ISBN 978-0-323-26339-9. First written by Lauren Ackerman in 1953 and carried forward by Juan Rosai.
  • Dabbs DJ. Diagnostic Immunohistochemistry: Theranostic and Genomic Applications. Elsevier. The standard dedicated reference for the subject of this tool.
  • Mills SE. Sternberg's Diagnostic Surgical Pathology. Wolters Kluwer. The other major general text.
  • Reddy VB. Gattuso's Differential Diagnosis in Surgical Pathology. 4th edition, Elsevier, 2022. ISBN 978-0-323-66165-2. Organised by differential rather than by diagnosis, which is the same way this tool is organised.
  • Odze RD, Goldblum JR. Surgical Pathology of the GI Tract, Liver, Biliary Tract and Pancreas. 4th edition, Elsevier, 2023. ISBN 978-0-323-67988-6.
  • Perry A, Brat DJ. Practical Surgical Neuropathology: A Diagnostic Approach. 2nd edition, Elsevier, 2018. ISBN 978-0-323-44941-0.
  • PathologyOutlines.com. Free, continuously updated, and the fastest place to check a marker or an entity at the microscope. pathologyoutlines.com
  • The Human Protein Atlas. Free. Shows the expression of a protein across normal tissues and tumours with the underlying images, which is useful when you want to know what a marker actually stains rather than what it is said to stain. proteinatlas.org

Content added beyond the references. A small number of entries were added from current diagnostic practice because the references predate them or do not cover them. ALK-positive histiocytosis is the clearest example: it is a 2022 WHO entity that appears in none of the three sources, and it was added after a reader tested the tool and found it missing. Those entries are flagged in their molecular line. Predictive markers, including PD-L1, ROS1 and SDHB, were added for the same reason.

A note on what sits behind all three. These are secondary sources. Each of them distils the WHO Classification of Tumours series and the primary literature, and Dr Schaberg says so explicitly of his own notes. The WHO volumes are the authority, and this tool has not yet been checked against them. Treat what follows as a reasoning exercise built on teaching material, not as a reference standard.

Anyone building on these sources should buy them. Two are commercial books and the third is given away by a working pathologist. None of them are reproduced here and none of them should be replaced by this.

Not a diagnostic device

This is a teaching tool for pathology training. It does not make diagnoses. Its recommendations must be read together with the morphology, the clinical history, adequate controls and current diagnostic criteria, and they do not replace the judgement of a qualified pathologist. It must not be used to guide the management of a patient.

Creator

Creator

PathoPanel is built and maintained by Dr Nixon Dangol, a third year pathology resident in Kathmandu, Nepal.

It grew out of the observation that trainees learn which stains exist for a tumour long before they learn which stain to order next, and that the second skill is the one used every day at the microscope. The tool is an attempt to make that reasoning visible: to show the differential shifting as each feature is added, and to explain why one antibody was chosen over another that would have been just as positive.

The whole thing is a single HTML file with no dependencies and no server. It can be saved, opened offline, and edited by anyone who wants to change a weight or add an entity.

Report an error

The library will contain mistakes. Errors in a teaching tool propagate, so a correction is genuinely more useful than a compliment.

Submit errors to pathnixon@gmail.com

What helps most, in order. An entity that is missing rather than mis-ranked, since an absent diagnosis is the one failure the tool cannot detect on its own. An expected staining result that is wrong, naming the entity, the antibody and what it should be. A pitfall that is misleading or out of date. Please say which module the case was in.
